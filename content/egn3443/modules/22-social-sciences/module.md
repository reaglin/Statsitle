Psychologists, sociologists, economists and political scientists study people, and people are harder to measure than steel. An attitude, an ability or a mood cannot be put on a scale: it is asked about, in a survey or a test, and the answers carry error. Most social data are **observational**, so cause is hard to show. And the field has learned, in the **replication crisis**, how easily statistics can mislead when studies are small and analyses are flexible.

This module shows **how statistics is used in the social sciences and psychology**: polls, scales, experiments, test scores and the lessons of the replication crisis. It does not teach the methods again. Where a method was taught in Part I, the page says where. Where it belongs to Part II (factor analysis, multilevel models), the page explains only as much as the application needs, and Part II will cover it in full.

**The data in this module are illustrative**: made up for this book, with realistic sizes and patterns, and labelled as such. They are not results of real surveys or experiments.

This module has six topics and a module check. Each topic is an item with four lesson tabs (and My notes, for your own notes):

- **Learn** explains how the field uses the method, works a small case, and shows the Python and R.
- **Example** has an example to try, with a figure that moves as you change the data, and the worked example with every result and the code.
- **Exercise** gives you a new data set from the field, or questions. Work out the answers, type them in, and press **Check my answers**.
- **Reference** has the formulas, the field's vocabulary, the Python and R functions, and where to read more.

You need Part I's confidence intervals (Module 7), hypothesis tests and power (Module 8), two-sample tests (Modules 9 and 10) and correlation (Module 11). The module check, the last item, asks ten questions over all six outcomes. You need 80 % to pass, and you can try again.
