Economists and business analysts use the statistics of Part I every day, under names of their own. A demand curve is a regression. A sales forecast is a trend line with a prediction interval. A central bank's rule for its interest rate is a multiple regression. The risk of an investment is a standard deviation, and its "beta" is a regression slope. A web shop's A/B test is a test of two proportions.

This module shows **how statistics is used in business and economics**: the field's own questions, words and data. It does not teach the methods again. Where a method was taught in Part I, the page says where ("see item 11.2"), so you can go back to it. Where a method belongs to Part II (time series, for example), the page explains only as much as the application needs, and Part II will cover it in full.

**The data in this module are illustrative**: made up for this book, with realistic sizes and patterns, and labelled as such. They are not real prices, rates or returns. Each page names where real data of the same kind can be found.

This module has six topics and a module check. Each topic is an item with four lesson tabs (and My notes, for your own notes):

- **Learn** explains how the field uses the method, works a small case, and shows the Python and R.
- **Example** has an example to try, with a figure that moves as you change the data, and the worked example with every result and the code.
- **Exercise** gives you a new data set from the field. Work out the answers by hand, in Python or R, or in the Workbench, type them in, and press **Check my answers**.
- **Reference** has the formulas, the field's vocabulary, the Python and R functions, and where to read more.

You need Part I's regression (Module 11), multiple regression (Module 12), probability distributions (Modules 4 and 5) and the two-proportion test (item 10.5). The module check, the last item, asks ten questions over all six outcomes. You need 80 % to pass, and you can try again.
