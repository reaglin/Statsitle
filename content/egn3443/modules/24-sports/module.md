Sport is one of the most measured activities there is: every pitch, shot, pass and sprint is recorded. Since the 1970s, and famously since the Oakland Athletics' "moneyball" seasons of the early 2000s, teams have used statistics to find undervalued players, to judge how lucky a season was, and to decide in-game strategy. Broadcasters show win probabilities live, and sports scientists measure athletes to the hundredth of a second.

This module shows **how statistics is used in sport**: which numbers predict winning, how much of a season is luck, what a streak means, how ratings and odds are probabilities, and how to tell a real improvement in an athlete from measurement noise. It does not teach the methods again; where a method was taught in Part I, the page says where.

**The data in this module are illustrative**: made up for this book, with the sizes and patterns of real leagues, but the teams and players are not real. Each page names where real data can be found (box scores and season records are public). The page on odds treats them **as probability only**: it gives no betting advice, and it shows why the expected value of a bet is negative.

This module has six topics and a module check. Each topic is an item with four lesson tabs (and My notes, for your own notes):

- **Learn** explains how sport uses the method, works a small case, and shows the Python and R.
- **Example** has an example to try, with a figure that moves as you change the data, and the worked example with every result and the code.
- **Exercise** gives you a new data set, or questions. Work out the answers, type them in, and press **Check my answers**.
- **Reference** has the formulas, the field's vocabulary, the Python and R functions, and where to read more.

You need Part I's binomial and normal distributions (Modules 4 and 5), the chi-square test (item 8.5) and regression (Module 11). The module check, the last item, asks ten questions over all six outcomes. You need 80 % to pass, and you can try again.
